For years, creatine was filed away as a thing only young men at the gym cared about. That was a mistake. It is one of the most studied supplements in existence, and it is inexpensive. What has actually been understudied, as the research itself points out, is its use specifically in women [1] — which is exactly the gap the newer research below starts to close.

# What creatine actually does

Creatine helps your muscles produce energy quickly. That is why it supports strength and the ability to keep training as you age, which matters enormously for women: muscle tends to decline with age generally, and midlife is a reasonable place to start paying attention, whatever share of that decline turns out to be about hormones specifically versus time alone.

# The benefits beyond muscle

The newer and more interesting research looks past the gym. There is growing evidence that creatine may support bone, mood, and the mental sharpness that perimenopause can blur [1,2]. The brain, like muscle, is an energy-hungry organ, and creatine appears to help it work. For a woman thinking about both her strength and her focus through the change, that is a great deal of value from one humble powder.

# What to look for

A few simple pointers:

- The plain, unflavored monohydrate form is the one with decades of evidence behind it. The fancier, pricier versions have not been shown to work any better.
- It works best alongside strength training, since it supports the work rather than replacing it [2].
- The old worry that it harms healthy kidneys has not held up in the research [1,2]. If you have kidney concerns or take medications, check with your prescriber first, as you would with anything new.

# A calm bottom line

Creatine is one of the rare supplements that is cheap, well studied, and genuinely aligned with aging strong. It will not do the work for you, and it is no substitute for protein, sleep, and movement. Paired with those foundations, it is a small, sensible edge.
