## How the claims in this book are marked

Every substantive claim carries a small tag, set in small capitals, at the end of the sentence or of the particular claim it applies to. There are five.

**[shown in people]** Demonstrated in human trials.

**[associated only]** Observed in people, but observationally. A correlation, with cause not established.

**[animals only]** Demonstrated in living animals, almost always rodents, and not yet in humans. Often good science. Not the same as evidence about you.

**[tested, didn't hold]** Researchers ran a proper study of this, and it did not work. The result showed no effect, or an effect in the wrong direction from the one hoped for. This is a finding, not a gap. Somebody looked, and what they found was that the thing does not do what it is said to do. The real confusion is against *not studied*. Tested, didn't hold means somebody checked and it failed. Not studied means nobody checked. They aren't degrees of the same thing, and one isn't a softer version of the other. A product that has been tested and failed is very often sold as though nobody had gotten around to looking yet.

**[not studied]** Covers two situations under one tag. Sometimes no one has properly tested the question at all. Other times the claim is my own reasoning, stitched together from other evidence rather than drawn from a study of its own. Both are legitimate, and neither is a failure the way *tested, didn't hold* is, so as you read, notice which kind you're looking at.

Opinions and judgments carry no tag.

**† not in women your age.** It marks a claim whose evidence comes from people older or younger than you, or from men, or from some other group you don't belong to. In the text the dagger usually appears on its own, tucked inside the bracket, as in **[shown in people †]**. It means the same thing every time.

**The count.** This book makes 49 tagged claims. Thirty-eight are shown in people, ten of those drawn from a group you may not belong to. Two are associated only. Four are animals only. Two are tested, didn't hold. Three are not studied. Ten claims in forty-nine, roughly one in five, carry the dagger.

**How to weigh them.** Use the tags this way as you read. When you see *shown in people* with no flag attached, give it the most weight. When you see *tested, didn't hold*, give it the least: that one has already been checked and found wanting, and no amount of hope should move it back up the list. When you see any other tag, or the dagger, treat the claim as a possibility worth weighing rather than a settled fact, and decide for yourself how much it should move you.

## Part One — Why Your Labs Said Fine and Your Body Knows Better

### Chapter 1: The Blind Spot in a Normal Panel

> **Short Version**
>
> A standard physical checks fasting glucose, and sometimes A1C. Both were built to catch a number that has already climbed, not the years of compensation before it moves. By 2023, more than one in three menopausal women in America met the lab criteria for prediabetes, and close to a third carried an elevated number no doctor had ever mentioned to them. The compensation that lets insulin resistance build for years before either test moves has been traced in animal studies; it has not been followed directly in women over time.

You went in for the visit you'd been putting off. Blood drawn, box checked, a week of not thinking about it, then the portal notification: *results ready.* You opened it already flinching. Every value sat inside its own small grey reference range. Glucose: normal. If there was a note at all, it said something like *no action needed.*

You closed the app and went back to being tired in a way sleep didn't fix, thirsty in a way that annoyed you before lunch, and a little wider at the waist than the version of you the scale still remembered. Nothing on the page explained any of that. The page wasn't wrong. It simply wasn't asked the right question.

A standard physical checks fasting glucose, and if your doctor thinks to add it, an A1C. Both are real tests, measuring real things. But they were built to catch two specific, late-stage failures: a fasting number that has already climbed, or a three-month average that has already been elevated long enough to show. Neither one is designed to catch the years of quiet drift that happen *before* either number moves: the stretch where your body is working harder than it used to, just to hold those two numbers where they are.

That drift has a name, insulin resistance, and by the time fasting glucose or A1C finally register it, your body has often been compensating for years. That's the compensation period, and it's exactly where you're most likely sitting right now: a lab report that says you're fine, while parts of you disagree.

**How common is this, really?**

Here is the finding behind that gap, and it's recent enough that most of the doctors seeing you haven't caught up to it yet. A 2026 analysis of twenty years of continuous national health survey data, the same government dataset used to track diabetes across the country, followed 3,574 naturally menopausal women aged 45 and older from 2003 through 2023. **[shown in people]**

Two things moved during that window. Diagnosed diabetes rose only modestly, from 11.3% to 11.9% of menopausal women. Prediabetes, the stage before that where fasting glucose or A1C has started to drift upward but hasn't crossed into diabetes, rose substantially, from 24.3% to 35.2%. By 2023, more than one in three menopausal women in America met the lab criteria for prediabetes.

The number that matters most here is a third one. The researchers also tracked something they called *unrecognized hyperglycemia*: women whose glucose or A1C already sat in the prediabetes or diabetes range, but who told the survey no doctor had ever mentioned it to them. That figure rose from 23.9% to 32.9% over the same twenty years. The number was there, on the page, and it didn't get flagged, explained, or followed up, despite every woman in that group having actually been tested. **[shown in people]** The gap itself, a third of menopausal women carrying an elevated number nobody named, is real, drawn from a large national sample. Whether catching it earlier actually changes what happens next is a different question. That one is **[not studied]**, by this survey or, as you'll see, by most of the field so far.

That distinction, between what a finding actually shows and what it's tempting to assume it shows, comes up again and again in the chapters ahead. Here, what's shown is the gap itself. What's not yet shown is exactly how much earlier recognition changes what happens next. Plausible, not proven. You deserve to know the difference, and you'll get it, claim by claim, chapter by chapter.

**The four parts ahead**

Part One walks through why the diagnostic line sits where it does, what's actually happening to insulin sensitivity as estrogen shifts through your forties and fifties, and exactly what a standard physical was, and wasn't, built to catch. Part Two hands you the tools to read your own numbers, including the ones your last physical didn't order. Part Three covers what the evidence actually supports for moving those numbers, graded the same way throughout. Part Four meets you wherever you're standing: watching a borderline number, already on a medication, in the middle of the transition, or afraid of a number that runs in your family.

None of it will tell you that your last normal panel lied to you. It didn't; it simply wasn't looking in the right place.

### Chapter 2: What Prediabetes Actually Means, and Why the Line Is Drawn Where It Is

> **Short Version**
>
> The same blood draw can be prediabetes under one standard and normal under another. The American Diabetes Association calls fasting glucose from 100 to 125 impaired; the World Health Organization starts at 110. The A1C range in wide use today, 5.7 to 6.4 percent, sits below where the 2009 expert committee that proposed it originally drew the line. Progression rates to diabetes differ depending on which definition diagnosed you. The threshold is a negotiated point on a slope, and it has moved before.

The same blood draw, run through the same lab, can be "prediabetes" in one country and "normal" in another, simply because two different medical bodies drew the fasting-glucose line in two different places, and neither line has been retired. The American Diabetes Association calls fasting glucose from 100 to 125 mg/dL impaired. The World Health Organization's threshold starts higher, at 110. If your result landed between those two numbers, you have simultaneously qualified and not qualified for the same label, and both organizations would defend their line.

That isn't a scandal. A threshold is just a point on a slope that a committee agreed was worth naming. Understanding that is the difference between panicking at a word and knowing what to actually do with it.

**Where the A1C line came from**

The fasting-glucose threshold has been argued over for decades, but the newer number, A1C, has an even shorter, more traceable history. In 2009, a group calling itself the International Expert Committee, made up of representatives from the ADA, the European Association for the Study of Diabetes, and the International Diabetes Federation, published a report in *Diabetes Care* proposing something that hadn't been standard before: using the A1C test, originally built to track blood sugar control in people already diagnosed, to diagnose diabetes in the first place. Their report identified an A1C of 6.0% to 6.4% as a category worth flagging as elevated risk. **[shown in people]** An expert consensus report, not a trial.

The proposal wasn't uncontested. That same year, other researchers in the field published a formal response in the same journal, pushing back on parts of the committee's reasoning. The number was debated by the people who study it, not simply handed down. The clinical range in wide use today, 5.7% to 6.4% for prediabetes, sits a little below where the original 2009 report drew its line. The number you saw on your own lab report has moved before, among the very people responsible for setting it.

None of this means the test is unreliable, or that the label doesn't matter. It means the label is a drawn line on a continuous slope, redrawn more than once already. Keep that in mind before letting three decimal points define how alarmed you are.

**Does the line actually predict anything?**

Here the evidence gets more solid, and more useful. A 2013 meta-analysis pooled 70 prospective studies: people diagnosed with some form of prediabetes, then followed over time to see who actually went on to develop type 2 diabetes. **[shown in people]** A meta-analysis of prospective data, about as strong an evidence tier as this chapter has to offer. It found that people's future risk did track meaningfully with prediabetes status, but the exact rate of progression depended on *which* definition had been used to diagnose them in the first place. People diagnosed by the ADA's fasting-glucose criteria progressed to diabetes at about 35.5 cases per 1,000 person-years. People diagnosed by the WHO's stricter fasting-glucose criteria progressed at roughly 47.4 per 1,000 person-years, a meaningfully higher rate, from a test drawing the line in a different place. People flagged by the A1C 6.0–6.4% range progressed at about 35.6 per 1,000 person-years, almost identical to the ADA fasting-glucose group.

The various prediabetes definitions pick up something real, even though they aren't interchangeable with each other. A body that crosses any of these lines is, on average, genuinely more likely to develop diabetes than one that doesn't. The line is imperfect and negotiated, and it still carries real information. Both things are true at once, and holding both is more useful than picking a side.

**What this means for you, specifically**

If your result came back "borderline," or your A1C sat at 5.6%, one-tenth of a point under the line, the tidy grey box on your lab report is doing you a disservice. It presents a hard edge where the actual science shows a slope. A result just under the threshold is still meaningful; it's a data point on the same slope as a result just over it, and the difference between the two may be smaller than the difference between how they're treated in the room.

That's the case for taking a "normal, but just barely" result as seriously as a flagged one, with the same attention rather than alarm. The next chapter looks at *why* that slope tends to move in a particular direction for women specifically, starting in your forties and fifties, and what's actually driving it.

### Chapter 3: Estrogen, Insulin, and the Transition Nobody Explained

> **Short Version**
>
> Before menopause, women have a measurable insulin-sensitivity advantage over men the same age, and estrogen is part of why, through a mechanism traced in mice affecting how much glucose the liver releases, not yet confirmed the same way in women. Two separate meta-analyses of hormone therapy trials, two decades apart, both found it lowers insulin resistance scores in postmenopausal women, with estrogen alone outperforming estrogen combined with a progestogen. That human effect is real and modest rather than a reversal, and whether hormone therapy suits you depends on your own history and risk factors.

Here's a fact most women are never told, because there's rarely a reason to mention it until it stops being true: before menopause, women have a real, measurable insulin-sensitivity advantage over men their own age, and a lower rate of type 2 diabetes to go with it. Researchers studying why landed on a specific hormone doing specific work, and on what happens in its absence. **[animals only]** A mouse model, described below.

**What the mechanism actually is**

A 2018 study out of Texas A&M investigated the question directly, using mice with and without ovaries, and mice engineered to lack a particular liver protein called Foxo1. Foxo1's job, broadly, is to help control how much glucose the liver releases into the bloodstream. In both male mice and mice with their ovaries removed, giving back estrogen improved insulin sensitivity and suppressed the liver's glucose output, but only when Foxo1 was present and functioning. In mice engineered without it, estrogen's benefit vanished. That let the researchers trace a specific chain: estrogen activates a receptor, which activates a signaling pathway, which acts on Foxo1, which throttles back how much sugar the liver dumps into the blood. **[animals only]** This traces the mechanism precisely, but it is a mouse liver, not a woman's. Mechanism plausibility is not the same as proof it works this way in you.

That's the *why*: a specific, traceable biological reason estrogen would be expected to help regulate blood sugar. It doesn't, on its own, tell you what happens in an actual woman's body when her own estrogen declines, or what happens if she takes estrogen back. For that, the mouse data has to hand off to human trials.

**What happens in actual women**

Two separate meta-analyses have pooled the human hormone-therapy trials and asked the same question: does giving postmenopausal women estrogen measurably change their insulin resistance, not just in theory but on the lab test?

The larger, older one pooled 107 randomized trials through 2004. In women without diabetes, hormone therapy reduced abdominal fat by 6.8%, reduced HOMA-IR, the standard insulin-resistance score, by 12.9%, and cut the relative risk of developing new-onset diabetes to 0.7 (a 30% reduction). In women who already had diabetes, the effect on HOMA-IR was even larger, a 35.8% reduction. **[shown in people]** A meta-analysis of RCTs, 107 trials.

A newer, more targeted analysis published in 2025 pooled 17 randomized trials specifically measuring HOMA-IR, covering 5,772 non-diabetic postmenopausal women, average age just under 57. It found the same direction of effect (hormone therapy lowered HOMA-IR significantly) and added a detail the older analysis didn't isolate: estrogen alone produced roughly three times the improvement of estrogen combined with a progestogen. **[shown in people]** A meta-analysis of RCTs, 17 trials, 5,772 women.

Two independently conducted meta-analyses, two decades apart, using different trial pools, landing on the same direction of effect. That's about as solid as human evidence gets in this field without a single, enormous dedicated trial, and for a question this specific, that trial doesn't exist and likely won't.

**The limits of this finding**

It would be easy to read the last two paragraphs as "take estrogen, fix your blood sugar." That's not what the data shows.

The effect is real, drawn from two separate human meta-analyses rather than a theory, and it's genuinely modest: a 12 to 13 percent reduction in a resistance score, not a reversal. Whether hormone therapy makes sense for you depends on your own history, risk factors, other menopause symptoms, and a conversation with your own prescriber, which is the point this keeps circling back to. A woman with a personal or family history that makes hormone therapy inadvisable for her isn't excluded from managing her blood sugar; she just has more reasons to lean on the chapters ahead (muscle, food, sleep) where the tools don't require a prescription at all.

The real use of this finding is smaller and more useful than a recommendation: it's an answer to *why now*. If you've felt your body handling food differently in your forties or fifties than it did at thirty, this is a real, mechanistically traced, human-trial-supported reason, not a sign that you let something slip.

The next chapter looks at what a standard yearly physical was built to catch, and why this whole shift can move through you for years before any of it shows up on the version of the test you're used to getting.

### Chapter 4: What a Standard Physical Wasn't Built to Catch

> **Short Version**
>
> Routine diabetes screening isn't universal. US guidelines target adults 35 to 70 who have overweight or obesity, which leaves out a woman at a BMI of 23, a woman of 33, and a woman of 72. Even when the test is ordered, your pancreas compensates by producing more insulin, holding fasting glucose in the normal range while the resistance underneath it builds, a pattern traced directly in mouse studies of the compensation mechanism. The test reports the outcome of that compensation, and moves only once it starts to fail.

The ritual feels thorough. Cuff on your arm, stethoscope on your chest, a vial or two of blood sent off to a lab that will, a few days later, hand back a page of numbers with the word "normal" printed next to most of them. It's reasonable to walk out of that room believing you've been checked for everything worth checking. You haven't, and not because anyone was careless: the checking itself has a narrower job than it looks like it does.

**Who actually gets screened**

Start with a fact that surprises most women when they hear it: routine screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes isn't universal. It's targeted. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, the body whose recommendations largely determine what your insurance covers as preventive care and what your doctor is prompted to order, currently recommends diabetes and prediabetes screening specifically for adults aged 35 to 70 who have overweight or obesity, defined by BMI. **[shown in people]** This is a clinical practice guideline built on a systematic review of the evidence, not a trial itself, but it directly determines who gets tested.

Read that again with an eye to who it leaves out. A woman at a BMI of 23 doesn't meet the criteria. A woman at 33 has met her weight criteria but isn't 35 yet. A woman at 72 has aged out of the recommendation entirely. None of those women are protected from insulin resistance by falling outside this window (the biology in Chapter 3 doesn't check anyone's BMI or birthday first), but none of them are guaranteed a fasting glucose test as a matter of routine screening protocol, either. If your doctor orders one anyway, it's often because of family history, a specific symptom, or simple thoroughness, not because the guideline requires it.

**Why the test can stay quiet even when it's ordered**

Now take the more surprising half of this: even a woman who *is* screened, whose blood is drawn and glucose measured, can have real, emerging insulin resistance and still see a normal number on the page. This isn't a flaw in the test. It's a feature of how your body responds to the problem before the problem becomes visible on that particular test.

When your cells start responding less readily to insulin, your pancreas doesn't just sit there and let glucose climb. It compensates: producing more insulin, and producing it more efficiently, to force glucose back down to a normal range. A 2016 study using live imaging of pancreatic islet cells traced exactly how this compensation happens: as insulin resistance emerged, the insulin-producing cells didn't primarily grow in number to keep up. They adapted functionally, changing their internal calcium signaling to become more efficient at their existing job, and this functional adaptation, not cell growth, was what held blood sugar in the normal range through the early stages. It was only as the animals moved into the prediabetic range that this functional compensation itself began to falter. **[animals only]** An imaging study in mice. This traces the mechanism precisely, and it matches what's long been understood clinically about how diabetes develops in stages, but it is not itself a study of women's blood sugar.

That's the piece missing from the mental picture most of us carry. It isn't that a fasting glucose test lies. It's that your pancreas is, for a real stretch of time, working harder behind the scenes specifically so that test keeps coming back normal. The test is reporting the outcome of a fight your body is currently winning, not the fight itself. Fasting glucose and A1C are both downstream of that compensation. They move only after the compensation starts to lose ground, which is later than most women assume.

**Putting Part One together**

This part has covered four separate reasons that lead to the same conclusion: the number on your last "normal" panel wasn't wrong, and it also wasn't looking where you needed it to look. It's built to catch a late-stage failure, not the compensation stage that can precede it by years. The line marking "prediabetes" has moved before and is drawn differently by different organizations, which tells you it's a threshold, not a cliff edge. Your own hormonal transition is a real, mechanistically traced reason your body's compensation may be working harder right now than it did a decade ago. And whether you're even flagged for screening in the first place depends on a BMI-and-age window that has nothing to do with whether the biology applies to you.

None of that is a reason for alarm. It's the reason the tools in Part Two matter. Part Two hands you the specific tools, the actual tests, what each one measures, and where each one still falls short on its own, to look earlier than a standard physical was built to look.

## Part Two — Reading the Numbers Yourself

### Chapter 5: A1C, Fasting Glucose, Fasting Insulin, HOMA-IR, OGTT: What Each One Actually Measures, and Where Each One Fails Alone

> **Short Version**
>
> A1C averages roughly three months of blood sugar and can run falsely high if your iron is low, an effect clearly shown in some populations and, in the one study that looked specifically at non-pregnant women, only a trend that did not reach significance. Fasting glucose is one snapshot and can stay normal for years. Fasting insulin shows how hard your body is working to keep it there, and is the number rarely ordered. HOMA-IR combines glucose and insulin into an estimate carrying real imprecision. The OGTT is the only one that watches your body handle an actual glucose load, and it is the one a routine visit is least likely to order.

Five numbers show up again and again in this book, and almost nobody has ever sat you down and explained what each one is actually built to catch, or what it quietly can't. None of them is useless. None of them is complete on its own, either. Here's what each one is really doing.

**A1C**

A1C measures the percentage of your hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells, that has glucose permanently stuck to it. Because red blood cells live for roughly three months, A1C works as a rolling average of your blood sugar over that stretch, which is exactly why it doesn't require fasting and can't be thrown off by what you ate for breakfast.

Its blind spot is built into the same mechanism that makes it useful: A1C isn't really measuring sugar directly, it's measuring how long sugar has had to stick to red blood cells, and it assumes those cells are living a normal lifespan. Anything that shortens or lengthens that lifespan distorts the number without your blood sugar changing at all. Iron-deficiency anemia is the clearest example. In gastrectomy patients prone to iron-deficiency anemia, A1C ran significantly higher than in matched patients without anemia, despite both groups having statistically identical fasting glucose and OGTT results. Ferritin itself correlated negatively with A1C: the lower the iron stores, the more inflated the A1C reading, independent of actual blood sugar. **[shown in people †]** A specific population, gastrectomy patients with iron-deficiency anemia, not midlife women generally. The mechanism, red blood cell lifespan, applies to anyone with iron deficiency; the size of the effect in a typical midlife woman with mild, undiagnosed low iron hasn't been separately studied. A separate study found the same false-elevation pattern reached statistical significance in pregnant women with iron deficiency, but only trended, without reaching significance, in non-pregnant women with the same deficiency. **[shown in people †]** Mixed significance by population. Your A1C could be running artificially high if your iron is also low; that's a question for your clinician, not an assumption to make on your own.

**Fasting glucose**

This one is exactly what it sounds like: a single measurement, taken at a single moment, after roughly eight hours without food. Its strength is also its limitation: it's a snapshot, not an average, and Chapter 4 already covered the mechanism that can hold this specific snapshot in the "normal" range for years, your pancreas compensating by producing more insulin, quietly, behind a number that looks fine.

**Fasting insulin**

This is the number almost nobody orders, and it's the one that can reveal the compensation itself rather than just its outcome. Where fasting glucose tells you whether your body is *currently succeeding* at holding blood sugar steady, fasting insulin tells you *how hard it's working* to do that. A high-normal or elevated fasting insulin, sitting next to a perfectly normal fasting glucose, can be the earliest visible sign that the compensation described in Chapter 4 is already underway.

Its practical limitation is one you won't see coming: fasting insulin has never been standardized across labs and populations the way glucose has. A 2026 study in Nepal set out to establish local reference ranges for fasting insulin specifically because the ranges in common use there had simply been imported from Western studies, without confirmation that they applied to a different population's physiology. **[shown in people]** This is a study about reference-range validity, not blood sugar outcomes. The point isn't the specific numbers; it's that "reference range" for this particular test is a more negotiable, less universal thing than for glucose. If your lab flags your fasting insulin as normal or abnormal, ask what reference range they're using and where it came from: a question that would sound strange asked about a glucose test, and is completely reasonable asked about this one.

**HOMA-IR**

HOMA-IR isn't a separate blood draw. It's fasting glucose and fasting insulin, multiplied together and divided by a constant, producing a single score that estimates how resistant your cells are to insulin's effect. It was developed in 1985 and validated against the gold-standard method for directly measuring insulin resistance, the euglycemic clamp, a demanding, hours-long procedure no one gets as a matter of routine care. The correlation between HOMA-IR and the clamp was strong (Rs = 0.88), and the correlation with fasting insulin alone, notably, was almost as strong (Rs = 0.81), suggesting fasting insulin is doing most of the real work in the formula. **[shown in people]** Validated against the clinical gold standard, though on a modest sample by today's standards. The same original paper was candid about a real limitation: the precision of the individual estimate carries a coefficient of variation around 31%, meaning any single person's HOMA-IR score has real "wobble" built into it. **[shown in people]** Treat one HOMA-IR number as a useful estimate, not a precise verdict.

There's also a population wrinkle: a small study comparing HOMA-IR against clamp measurements in South Asian and Caucasian adults found the index correlated well with the clamp in the South Asian group, but not in the Caucasian group, while fasting insulin itself was substantially higher in South Asian participants despite both groups having nearly identical fasting glucose. **[shown in people †]** Small sample. The ethnicity-specific validity point is real; the direction and size of it in other populations, including midlife women generally, isn't established from this study alone.

**Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)**

The OGTT asks your body to do something the other four tests never do: actually process a real glucose load, and shows how you handle it over the following one to two hours, not just where you start. It's the closest a clinic visit gets to the euglycemic clamp without the clamp itself, and it can catch a category of trouble, impaired glucose *tolerance*, as opposed to impaired fasting glucose, that a fasting-only test structurally cannot see, because the fasting test never asks your body to handle a real glucose challenge in the first place.

Even the OGTT, done in its usual two-point form, may not be capturing everything worth capturing. A twelve-year study following adults in Hong Kong found that a high glucose reading at the one-hour mark of the OGTT, a measurement most labs don't report and most doctors don't ask for, predicted future type 2 diabetes independent of standard fasting and two-hour results, and identified nearly a quarter of the cohort as high-risk despite those same people testing as normal by the tests actually used to diagnose them. **[shown in people †]** A genuine, prospective, twelve-year finding, in a Hong Kong Chinese cohort. It hasn't yet changed standard clinical practice, so don't expect your lab to report it; it's a preview of where this test may be headed, not a number you can currently ask for by name.

**Taken together**

No single one of these five is the answer. A1C can run artificially high with iron deficiency and misses the picture entirely if drawn alone. Fasting glucose can look pristine while your pancreas works overtime behind it. Fasting insulin reveals that effort but comes with reference ranges that vary by lab and population. HOMA-IR combines the two into a useful estimate with real, acknowledged imprecision. And the OGTT, the most physiologically informative of the five, still may not be extracting everything from its own data that it could.

Run together, though, they triangulate on the truth in a way none of them can alone, which is exactly the case for asking for more than one. The next chapter gives you the specific panel to ask for, and the words to ask for it with.

### Chapter 6: The Panel to Ask For

> **Short Version**
>
> Four numbers from a single draw: fasting glucose, A1C, fasting insulin, and a HOMA-IR calculated from the last two. Ask about an oral glucose tolerance test as well if you have a family history, a history of gestational diabetes, PCOS, or a previous borderline result. Fasting insulin sits outside standard screening guidelines, so whether it's covered varies by plan and by how the order is coded. Ask what it costs before the draw rather than after.

Everything so far has explained why. This chapter is the how.

**The panel itself**

Four numbers, all from one blood draw: fasting glucose, A1C, fasting insulin, and a calculated HOMA-IR score (this last one isn't a separate test: it's fasting glucose and fasting insulin run through the formula from Chapter 5, and either your lab or your doctor's office can calculate it once both numbers exist). If you have a personal or family history that raises your risk (a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes, a personal history of gestational diabetes, PCOS, or a fasting number that's come back borderline before), raise whether an OGTT makes sense too, for the reason Chapter 5 laid out: it's the one test here that actually watches your body handle a real glucose load, not just where you start.

Gestational diabetes deserves a specific mention here, because its consequences don't end at delivery. A large 2022 meta-analysis pooling data on more than half a million women with a history of gestational diabetes, against more than eight million without, found a 45% increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease overall, even when the analysis was narrowed to women who never went on to develop diabetes at all. **[shown in people]** A meta-analysis of observational studies; the authors rated the certainty of evidence low to very low, a real limitation, not a reason to dismiss the finding. If gestational diabetes is anywhere in your history, it belongs in this conversation whether or not you were ever told to think of it as an ongoing risk factor.

**Why you may need to ask rather than wait**

Chapter 4 covered this, and it changes how you walk into the appointment: routine screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes is currently recommended, as a matter of guideline-driven practice, specifically for adults 35 to 70 with a BMI of 25 or higher. If you fall outside that window (younger, older, or a BMI under 25), your fasting glucose may simply never come up unless you're the one who raises it. That's not a flaw in your doctor's judgment. It's a gap in what the guideline covers, and you're allowed to widen it for yourself.

Fasting insulin sits in a further gap of its own: it isn't part of any standard screening guideline the way fasting glucose and A1C are, which means whether it's covered by your insurance as routine preventive care varies by plan, by lab, and by how your doctor codes the order. Ask your office directly what it would cost out of pocket if it isn't covered, before assuming either way: a five-minute question that saves a surprise on the bill.

**What to actually say**

You don't need medical language to ask for this well. A version of the following, adapted to your own situation, does the job:

*"I'd like to ask for a fasting insulin level alongside my glucose and A1C at my next blood draw, and to have HOMA-IR calculated from the results. I've been reading about how insulin resistance can develop years before glucose or A1C show it, and I'd rather catch it early than wait for it to show up on the tests I'd normally get."*

If a risk factor applies to you (family history, prior gestational diabetes, PCOS, a previous borderline result), add it directly: *"Given [your specific history], I'd also like to talk about whether an oral glucose tolerance test makes sense for me."*

If the answer is no, or "that's not necessary yet," that's useful information too. It may mean your particular risk profile genuinely doesn't call for it right now, or it may mean the request needs to go to a different clinician, or a direct-access lab that lets you order select tests yourself without a full office visit. Either way, you'll know you asked, on purpose, rather than waited to be offered.

**What the results actually tell you**

Once you have all four numbers, Chapter 5 is your reference for reading them, not in isolation, but against each other. A normal fasting glucose next to a high-normal fasting insulin is not "you're fine, ignore the second number." It's the exact pattern Chapter 4 described: a body compensating well enough that the older test still passes, while the newer one is already telling you what that compensation is costing. That combination, more than any single number on the page, is what the last two chapters have been building toward helping you read.

The next chapter takes on the tool everyone's heard of and almost nobody has real evidence for in your situation: continuous glucose monitors, and what's actually been shown about wearing one when you don't have diabetes.

### Chapter 7: Continuous Glucose Monitors: What's Shown, What's Sold

> **Short Version**
>
> In healthy nondiabetic adults wearing a research-grade sensor, mean glucose ran 98 to 99 mg/dL, and they spent a median 96 percent of the time between 70 and 140. A rise after a meal is the system working. The estimated A1C most consumer apps display ran 0.59 percentage points above real lab values, with a clinically meaningful gap in 71 percent of healthy participants. Whether wearing one changes any outcome for someone without diabetes hasn't been tested in a randomized trial.

Of every tool in this space, this is the one you've most likely already seen someone wear: a small sensor on the back of an arm, paired to a phone app promising real-time insight into "how your body actually responds to food." It's also the tool with the widest gap between what's marketed and what's been measured. This chapter closes that gap in both directions: what a CGM genuinely shows you, and what its most popular feature is currently getting wrong.

**What "normal" actually looks like on a sensor**

Start with something almost no CGM marketing shows you: an actual reference dataset for what a healthy, nondiabetic person's glucose looks like on a modern sensor. A 2019 multicenter study fitted 153 healthy, nondiabetic volunteers (ages 7 to 80, no diabetes, no obesity) with a blinded continuous glucose monitor for ten days and simply recorded what happened. Mean glucose ran 98 to 99 mg/dL across most age groups, ticking up slightly to 104 mg/dL in participants over 60. These healthy people spent a median of 96% of their time in the 70 to 140 mg/dL range, with a median of only about 30 minutes a day above 140. **[shown in people]** A real, multicenter reference dataset, though skewed toward participants with nonobese BMI, so it may not fully represent every reader's baseline.

That matters because it means an app showing you an occasional spike above 140 after a meal isn't showing you a malfunction. Chapter 5's OGTT discussion already established that healthy bodies are expected to rise and fall with food; that's the system working. Real reference data like this is the thing to hold the app's alarm bells against, not your gut feeling about whether a graph line looks alarming.

**The feature to distrust the most**

Most consumer CGM apps offer a headline number alongside the raw glucose graph: an "estimated A1C," calculated directly from your sensor readings and often called a Glucose Management Indicator, or GMI. It's presented, implicitly or explicitly, as a preview of what a lab would tell you. A follow-up study from the same research group, using the same 153 healthy nondiabetic participants, tested exactly how well that estimate held up against each person's actual lab-drawn A1C. It didn't hold up well. On average, the CGM's estimated A1C ran 0.59 percentage points *higher* than the real lab value, and a clinically meaningful gap of 0.4 points or more showed up in 71% of these healthy participants, nearly double the mismatch rate seen when the GMI formula was originally built and tested. **[shown in people]** Same research-grade device and rigorous protocol as the reference-range study above. This is a real, measured discordance, not a theoretical concern.

If you or a friend has worn a consumer CGM and been quietly alarmed by an "estimated A1C" that read higher than expected, the evidence says that alarm may be measuring the algorithm's own bias more than your actual blood sugar. It's a number presented with medical-sounding confidence that the evidence shows runs systematically hot in people who don't have diabetes.

**Does wearing one actually change anything?**

Here's the bigger, harder question, and the one the marketing doesn't answer: for someone without diabetes, does wearing a CGM and watching the graph actually lead to better blood sugar, better weight, or any other measured outcome, or does it just produce more data to look at? A 2025 review examining exactly this question, specifically for CGM combined with the newer weight-loss medications in people who have obesity but not diabetes, found something stark: no randomized controlled trials have tested this combination at all. The reviewers called it "a critical evidence gap," and noted that the CGM-derived metrics themselves remain unstandardized in this context, meaning even the numbers the device produces aren't yet agreed upon, let alone proven to change what happens next. **[not studied]** This is the field's own researchers naming the gap, not an assumption made here.

That doesn't mean CGMs are useless in research. A small study of 40 healthy and prediabetic adults, tracking glucose alongside diet and step counts for two weeks, found real, measurable differences in glucose variability between the two groups; prediabetes was linked to significantly higher swings in blood sugar across the day. **[shown in people]** A small observational study. This shows CGMs can detect real physiological differences worth studying further. It does not show that an individual wearing one and adjusting their own behavior based on the graph produces better outcomes than not wearing one; that specific question hasn't been tested. The tool clearly works as a research instrument. Whether it works as a personal behavior-change device, for an individual without diabetes deciding what to eat for dinner based on a graph, is a different question, and it's one the evidence hasn't answered yet, no matter how confidently an app's marketing page answers it for you.

**What this means for you**

If you're curious and want to try one, that's a reasonable personal choice, and the reference data above gives you something real to compare your own graph against. What you shouldn't do is treat the "estimated A1C" as equivalent to a lab test, panic at a normal post-meal rise, or assume that wearing one is doing something for your long-term blood sugar that hasn't actually been measured yet. No book, program, or app claiming otherwise can currently back that claim with a trial.

Part Three turns from measuring the problem to what the evidence actually supports for moving it, starting with the connection between your muscle and your glucose, which turns out to be the strongest evidence gathered here.

## Part Three — What Actually Moves the Number

### Chapter 8: The Muscle Connection

> **Short Version**
>
> Muscle is the largest site for clearing glucose from your blood, and cell and animal research traces a second route into the cell that muscle contraction opens without needing insulin's permission at all. Across 77 randomized trials of lifestyle programs in prediabetes, those including supervised exercise training cut diabetes incidence to a relative risk of 0.40, against 0.69 for programs without it. Not every kind of movement moves every number: Nordic walking lowered A1C across six trials while leaving fasting glucose and HOMA-IR unchanged.

This chapter sits at the exact point where this territory and *The Muscle You Keep* overlap: the same tissue, asked about from two different directions. That book asks how you keep your muscle through midlife. This chapter asks something narrower: what does having it actually do to your blood sugar. The answer turns out to rest on one of the strongest, most consistently supported mechanisms in this book.

**Muscle is where the glucose goes**

Skeletal muscle is the largest site in your body for clearing glucose out of the bloodstream after insulin gives the signal. That part is old, settled physiology. What's more interesting, and less well known outside research circles, is that muscle has a *second* route for pulling glucose in, one that doesn't wait for insulin's permission at all.

A detailed 2026 review of the molecular pathways involved lays this out precisely: alongside the well-known insulin signaling route, muscle contraction itself activates a completely separate set of signals (through calcium release, mechanical and metabolic stress sensors, and an energy-sensing enzyme called AMPK) that independently pulls the same glucose transporter, GLUT4, to the surface of the muscle cell. **[animals only]** A mechanistic review synthesizing cell and animal-level signaling research, not itself new data. Even in a body where insulin's usual signal is being ignored, the physical act of a muscle contracting can still open a separate door and let glucose in. That's the mechanistic reason exercise works on blood sugar even in someone who is, by definition, resistant to insulin's usual message.

**A relationship that runs both ways**

The connection isn't one-directional. A 2025 review of creatine supplementation combined with exercise for preventing type 2 diabetes opens with a synthesis of exactly this cycle as its background: less muscle mass means fewer sites available to clear glucose, which worsens insulin resistance, which in turn accelerates further muscle loss, a cycle that can quietly compound through the same years covered in Chapter 3. **[associated only]** The individual mechanistic pieces are well established in lab and observational data; the full cycle as a sustained causal loop hasn't been demonstrated end-to-end in a trial. This is precisely the mechanism *The Muscle You Keep* is built around from the other side: protecting muscle isn't cosmetic maintenance, it's protecting one of your body's main tools for managing its own blood sugar.

**Does this actually change outcomes, at scale?**

Here's where the evidence moves from plausible mechanism to real, population-level proof. A 2026 meta-analysis and meta-regression pooled 77 randomized controlled trials of lifestyle interventions in adults with prediabetes (22,629 participants in total) specifically to identify which program components actually worked. Weight loss mattered, predictably. But interventions that included supervised exercise training reduced the incidence of diabetes far more than interventions without it: a relative risk of 0.40 with supervised exercise, compared to 0.69 without. **[shown in people]** A large, current meta-analysis and meta-regression of randomized trials, about as strong as evidence gets in this field. Put differently, adding structured exercise to a diabetes-prevention program didn't just help. It roughly doubled the program's effectiveness at actually preventing diabetes, relative to programs relying on diet and weight loss alone.

**Which exercise, and what it actually moves**

Not every form of movement moves every number. A 2026 meta-analysis of Nordic walking (a genuine, whole-body aerobic exercise) in adults with prediabetes or diabetes found it meaningfully reduced A1C, but had no measurable effect on fasting glucose or HOMA-IR. **[shown in people]** A meta-analysis of six RCTs, a small evidence base, worth reading as a real but limited finding rather than a verdict on aerobic exercise broadly. That's not a contradiction of anything above. It's a reminder that different exercise types move different markers, sometimes for reasons still being worked out, and a claim as broad as "exercise fixes blood sugar" deserves the same scrutiny as everything else in this chapter.

**Where this leaves you**

Muscle matters to your blood sugar specifically, and building or protecting it belongs in any real answer to what you do next. The protein targets, the training progressions, the specifics for a body already navigating midlife hormonal change: that's a full book's worth of work, and it's already been written. If protecting muscle for your blood sugar's sake, not just your strength, matters to you now, *The Muscle You Keep* is where to go next.

The next chapter turns to food, graded the same way, and measured against what the popular "glucose hacks" actually have evidence for, not just popularity.

### Chapter 9: Food, Graded Honestly

> **Short Version**
>
> Eating protein and vegetables before carbohydrate lowered the glucose response by 38.8 percent against carbohydrate first, tested in people with prediabetes specifically. Walking after each meal beat one daily walk, though that trial ran in people already diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. Apple cider vinegar's fasting-glucose and A1C results come entirely from diagnosed diabetes as well. And nighttime protein, tested directly, made the next morning's glucose worse.

*The Glucose Goddess Method* built an entire bestselling program around four "hacks" (food order, vinegar, a savory breakfast, movement after eating) presented in one uniform, confident tone, as though each carried the same weight of proof. It doesn't. Some of it holds up well. Some of it holds up only for a population most readers of this book aren't in. This chapter grades each one separately and without pretending the evidence is tidier than it is.

**Food order: the best-supported hack in this chapter**

Eating vegetables and protein before carbohydrate, rather than digging into the carbohydrate first, is the one popular "hack" with evidence that actually matches this book's readers. A 2018 trial tested exactly this in people with prediabetes specifically: not healthy volunteers, not diagnosed diabetics, prediabetes. Fifteen participants ate an identical meal on three separate days, varying only the order: carbohydrate first, protein-and-vegetables first, or vegetables first. Eating protein and vegetables before the carbohydrate lowered the glucose area-under-the-curve by 38.8% compared with eating carbohydrate first, and both non-carbohydrate-first orders blunted the glucose peak by more than 40%. **[shown in people]** A real randomized crossover trial, conducted in the exact population this book is written for: prediabetes, not diabetes, not healthy volunteers.

If you take away one "hack" from this chapter, this is the one with your name on it.

**Walking after meals: strong evidence, borrowed from a different population**

A well-designed 2016 randomized crossover study compared two ways of meeting the same physical activity guideline: 30 minutes of walking once a day, versus 10 minutes of walking after each main meal. Both technically satisfied the same recommendation. They did not produce the same result. Walking after meals significantly lowered postprandial glucose, most strikingly after the evening meal, when the most carbohydrate tends to be eaten and people are most likely to sit down afterward. **[shown in people †]** This trial was conducted in adults already diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, not people with prediabetes or normal glucose tolerance. The mechanism (Chapter 8's muscle-contraction pathway) has no obvious reason to work differently before a diagnosis than after one, but the specific claim, that walking after a meal measurably lowers *your* post-meal glucose, today, before any diagnosis, hasn't itself been tested in someone in your position. Plausible, not yet proven for you specifically.

**Vinegar: real in one population, oversold for this book's readers**

This is the hack that most needs careful treatment. A 2009 trial testing cinnamon and vinegar together found a real drop in blood glucose at the 15-minute mark, but by the end of the full two-hour measurement window, the overall glucose response was no different from the control meal. **[tested, didn't hold]** A real randomized trial, and the headline effect measured at one early timepoint did not survive across the full postprandial period.

Zoom out to the larger evidence base, though, and vinegar looks more credible than that one trial suggests, just not for the population reading this book. A 2023 network meta-analysis pooling 44 randomized trials and 3,130 participants found apple cider vinegar produced the largest fasting-glucose reduction of six herbs tested, and was one of only two that meaningfully lowered A1C. **[shown in people †]** Every trial in this network meta-analysis was conducted in people already diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. The size of any effect in prediabetes, or in a woman with normal labs trying to stay ahead of a diagnosis, has not been separately established.

Vinegar has real effects, just not proven ones for your situation yet. "A splash of vinegar before your pasta will flatten your glucose graph," said with the same confidence as the food-order finding above, runs ahead of where the evidence for *your* situation actually is. That gap between "shown in people with diabetes" and "shown in people like you" is exactly the distinction the dagger flag exists to make visible.

**A savory breakfast, and where the evidence stops**

The advice to favor a protein-forward breakfast over a sugary one is reasonable and consistent with the food-order evidence above: protein and vegetables ahead of refined carbohydrate, applied to the first meal of the day rather than a later one. Some popular advice extends this into claims about *nighttime* protein intake improving the next morning's blood sugar. At least one trial testing that specific version found the opposite of what was expected: a dose of whey protein taken in the middle of the night measurably *worsened* the following breakfast's glucose response. **[tested, didn't hold]** A real randomized crossover trial, and the specific claim tested, nighttime protein improving next-day glucose, didn't merely lack support. It ran backward. That's why the savory-breakfast advice here stops at the first meal of the day rather than extending into nighttime protein timing.

**The graded verdict**

Food order: well-supported, in your population, worth building a habit around. Walking after meals: strong evidence, one population removed from yours, reasonable to try on the strength of the mechanism. Vinegar: real effects exist, but so far only where they've actually been measured: in people with a diagnosis, not the readers here who are trying to stay ahead of one. None of that makes vinegar useless or food order magic. It makes them exactly what this whole approach has been about: claims you can check, not a program you're asked to trust.

### Chapter 10: Sleep, Stress, and the Numbers Nobody Mentions

> **Short Version**
>
> Four days at 4.5 hours in bed left insulin signaling in fat cells nearly three times weaker. Two weeks at 5.5 hours reduced both glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. Sleeping under six hours a night carries roughly 30 percent higher future diabetes risk, across more than 447,000 people. Menopause independently raises obstructive sleep apnea risk through hormonal effects on airway tone. Stress is harder to separate from everything that tends to arrive with it.

Food gets a chapter. Movement got its own chapter, plus a bridge to an entire other book. Sleep and stress usually get a bullet point at most, even though the evidence for them is real; neither one produces a clean before-and-after photo, which makes them easy to skip in most blood-sugar content. This chapter gives them the space the evidence actually earns.

**What losing sleep does to your insulin, directly measured**

Two small, tightly controlled experimental studies get at this question directly, controlling exactly how much sleep healthy volunteers got and measuring what happened to their insulin sensitivity, rather than simply asking people how they slept and checking their labs years later.

In the first, seven healthy adults spent four days getting either 8.5 hours or 4.5 hours in bed, in random order, with food intake and activity held constant. Fat-cell biopsies taken after each condition showed that insulin's ability to trigger its normal cellular signal was nearly three times weaker after sleep restriction than after normal sleep, and whole-body insulin sensitivity, measured directly, dropped in parallel. **[shown in people]** A real experimental crossover study, though a small one (seven participants), so treat the exact size of the effect as a signal, not a precise number to bank on.

The second, larger version ran the experiment for two full weeks rather than four days: eleven healthy adults, averaging 39 years old, spent 14 days at a 5.5-hour bedtime and 14 days at 8.5 hours, in a controlled research setting. Recurrent sleep restriction reduced both glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, even though both conditions produced similar weight gain. Cortisol itself, measured over 24 hours, didn't change between conditions, but nighttime norepinephrine and 24-hour epinephrine, two other stress-response hormones, rose modestly during the short-sleep condition. **[shown in people]** Another real experimental crossover study, still a modest sample size, but the age range lands close to this book's own readers, and it's the cleanest human evidence in this chapter for the direct mechanism.

That last detail matters for the section on stress below: even in a lab tightly controlled enough to isolate sleep from everything else, blood sugar's stress-hormone response turned out to be more specific than "cortisol goes up." Keep that in mind before accepting any tidy story about which hormone is doing the damage.

**Does this actually predict who gets diabetes?**

The experimental studies show a real mechanism in a small number of people over days or weeks. A separate, much larger body of evidence asks the population-level question: does habitually short sleep predict who actually develops diabetes, years later, in real life? A study following 241,949 adults, later combined with nine other studies into a meta-analysis covering more than 447,000 people total, found that sleeping less than six hours a night, compared with seven, was associated with roughly a 30% higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes, a relationship that held even after excluding people with existing illness at the start and adjusting for baseline health, addressing the obvious concern that sick people just happen to sleep worse. **[shown in people]** A large prospective study plus meta-analysis, genuinely one of the more convincing population-level findings gathered here.

**Why menopause makes this harder, not just more annoying**

Sleep disruption in midlife is often filed under "hot flashes, obviously," and stops there. There's a second, less-discussed mechanism: declining estrogen and progesterone reduce the tone-supporting effect these hormones normally have on the upper airway, and a multidisciplinary consensus statement on sleep-disordered breathing in women identifies menopause as a genuine, independent driver of increased obstructive sleep apnea risk, a physical airway problem, not just a hormonal hot-flash problem. **[associated only]** A synthesized expert consensus statement drawing on existing epidemiology and physiology, not a trial of its own, but the underlying mechanism (hormone-dependent airway tone) is well established. Some of the "can't sleep through the night" of midlife has a structural component to raise with a clinician, not only a symptom to wait out.

**Stress: real, plausible, and harder to isolate than sleep**

It would be tidy to end this chapter with a clean cortisol story: stress raises cortisol, cortisol raises blood sugar, manage your stress and the number moves. Some of that is well established: glucocorticoids reliably raise blood glucose, which is precisely why people prescribed steroid medications are routinely monitored for it. But the leap from that pharmacological fact to "your day-to-day psychological stress is measurably raising your blood sugar right now" is a harder one to isolate cleanly in real life, for a simple reason: chronic stress rarely travels alone. It tends to arrive bundled with worse sleep, less appealing food choices, and less movement, which makes it genuinely difficult for research to credit stress itself, apart from everything stress tends to bring with it. **[associated only]** This is the actual state of the evidence for chronic psychological stress specifically, as distinct from the sleep-restriction studies above, which could isolate sleep alone.

What has been tested more directly is whether stress-reduction practices themselves move the needle. A 2025 review of yoga and meditation practices in people with type 2 diabetes found that practices like Pranayama, Asanas, and meditation were associated with reductions in inflammatory markers, several stress hormones, and improved HOMA-IR scores. **[shown in people †]** This is a narrative review, not a single controlled trial, and every study it draws on was conducted in people already diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, not prediabetes. It's a reasonable, low-risk thing to try on the strength of a plausible mechanism and a genuinely diabetes-diagnosed evidence base, not a proven fix for a reader who doesn't yet have that diagnosis.

**Sleep versus stress, side by side**

Sleep has the more direct evidence: two small experimental studies showing a real mechanism, and one large population study showing the real-world stakes, in numbers big enough to trust. Stress has the more intuitive story and the harder evidence to isolate: plausible, likely partly true, and genuinely tangled up with everything else stress brings along with it. Both belong in the same conversation as food and muscle, not as an afterthought to them.

### Chapter 11: The Supplement Aisle, Graded

> **Short Version**
>
> Berberine has real trial data behind it, all of it from people who already have type 2 diabetes, and two credible meta-analyses disagree on how large the effect is. Chromium has only ever been tested as an add-on to prescribed diabetes medication. Magnesium is the one item on this shelf tested directly in prediabetic adults, where it improved two-hour OGTT glucose and HOMA-IR modestly, with good tolerability and no significant change in fasting glucose.

Every bottle on this shelf is marketed with the same confidence, in the same font, at the same volume. The evidence behind them isn't nearly so uniform. This chapter grades three of the most commonly recommended blood-sugar supplements, and the one that comes out looking best is not the one getting the most attention online.

**Berberine: real evidence, in the wrong population, and even researchers don't agree how big the effect is**

Berberine has the loudest reputation of anything here, sold online as "nature's Ozempic," recommended in comment sections under every blood-sugar video. A 2021 meta-analysis pooling 46 randomized trials in people with type 2 diabetes found real reductions in HbA1c (–0.73 percentage points), fasting glucose, and HOMA-IR, alongside improvements in cholesterol markers. **[shown in people †]** A substantial meta-analysis, but every trial was conducted in people already diagnosed with type 2 diabetes: not prediabetes, not healthy adults trying to stay ahead of a diagnosis.

Here's the detail that matters more than the headline number: a newer 2026 meta-analysis, using a larger and more recent set of over 30 trials, arrived at a noticeably smaller effect, describing berberine's benefit as "statistically significant but clinically modest." **[shown in people †]** Also diagnosed type 2 diabetes only. The same paper ran a molecular-modeling side analysis finding that berberine binds its target digestive enzyme more weakly than the prescription drug it's often compared to. That part is computer modeling of a molecule rather than a measurement in any living body, so treat it as an explanation for the modest clinical numbers rather than as evidence of its own. Two credible meta-analyses, both real, land on different magnitudes for the same question, which is itself useful information. When even the researchers studying a supplement can't agree on how big its effect is, "nature's Ozempic" doesn't accurately describe where the evidence actually sits.

**Chromium: same story, and only ever tested as an add-on**

A large 2022 meta-analysis pooling 119 trials across nine different nutrient supplements, all used *alongside* prescribed diabetes medication in people with type 2 diabetes, found chromium significantly improved HbA1c, fasting glucose, and HOMA-IR. **[shown in people †]** Real evidence, but for a narrower claim than the marketing suggests: chromium as an add-on to drug therapy in diagnosed diabetes, not chromium taken alone by someone with prediabetes or normal labs. That's a meaningfully different question from the one most readers of this book are actually asking, and it hasn't been answered yet.

**Magnesium: the one actually tested on people like you**

This is the supplement in this chapter that gets to skip the dagger. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis looked specifically at oral magnesium supplementation in adults with prediabetes, the exact population this book is written for, not a borrowed population from a different diagnosis. Across five trials and 384 participants, fasting glucose didn't move significantly, but 2-hour OGTT glucose improved meaningfully, HOMA-IR improved, and triglycerides and HDL cholesterol both moved in a favorable direction, alongside excellent safety and tolerability. **[shown in people]** A real meta-analysis, conducted directly in prediabetic adults, with a genuinely plausible mechanism behind it: magnesium is a required cofactor in the cellular machinery insulin uses to signal, and mild magnesium insufficiency is common and frequently goes unmeasured on a standard panel.

The effect size here is modest, not dramatic, and it isn't a substitute for anything else covered so far. But it's the one supplement in this chapter with evidence actually drawn from people in your position, rather than evidence on loan from a diagnosis you don't have.

**The bottom line on this shelf**

Don't build a supplement stack out of evidence borrowed from a diagnosis you don't have. Berberine and chromium both have real trial data behind them, genuinely real, not manufactured, but that data describes what happens in people already managing type 2 diabetes, often alongside prescription medication, not what happens in a woman with prediabetes or a normal panel trying to stay ahead of one. Magnesium is the one item on this shelf tested in the actual population this book is for, with a modest but real result and one of the better safety profiles of anything sold as a supplement. If you're going to spend money in this aisle on the strength of this chapter, that's where the evidence actually points.

### Chapter 12: If You've Been Told to "Just Watch It"

> **Short Version**
>
> In a trial of 3,234 adults with elevated glucose, a structured lifestyle program cut diabetes incidence by 58 percent over 2.8 years. Metformin cut it by 31 percent. Ten years on, the lifestyle group still held the lowest cumulative incidence, a 34 percent reduction, though year-by-year rates converged once everyone was offered the same support. The benefit came from the practice continuing rather than from a single successful year.

"Let's just watch it" can land like a dismissal, the same non-answer as the normal panel from Chapter 1, delivered again with a slightly more concerned tone. It doesn't have to be one. "Watching it" happens to describe one of the best-tested interventions in all of preventive medicine, if you know what watching it well actually means. Most people are never told.

**The trial this chapter is built on**

In 2002, researchers randomly assigned 3,234 adults with elevated fasting and post-load glucose (nondiabetic, but clearly in the zone this book has been describing) to one of three groups: a placebo pill, metformin, or a structured lifestyle program aimed at 7% weight loss and 150 minutes of activity a week. The participants were, on average, 51 years old; 68% were women. Over an average follow-up of 2.8 years, the lifestyle group's diabetes incidence was reduced by 58% compared with placebo. Metformin reduced it by 31%, real, but significantly less than lifestyle change achieved on its own. To prevent one case of diabetes over three years, just under seven people needed to go through the lifestyle program. **[shown in people]** A large, landmark randomized controlled trial, one of the most influential in this field, and in a population close to this book's own readers in age and sex.

**Does it last, or does the benefit fade?**

This is the part most summaries skip. The same participants were followed for a full decade. Cumulative diabetes incidence over the ten years remained lowest in the group originally assigned to lifestyle intervention: a 34% reduction compared with the original placebo group, still standing a decade later. But there's a real nuance: once the trial ended and *everyone*, including the original placebo and metformin groups, was offered the same lifestyle support, the year-by-year rate of new diabetes cases converged across all three groups. **[shown in people]** The same landmark cohort, followed for ten years. The read that actually fits the data: the early head start from lifestyle change produces a durable, decade-long reduction in *cumulative* cases, genuinely real, not a mirage, but it isn't a one-time inoculation. The ongoing practice is what keeps producing the benefit, for whichever group is doing it, for as long as they keep doing it.

That's not a discouraging finding. It's a realistic one: "watch it" means something real when done this way, and it's not a single successful year that buys permanent immunity.

**What "watching it well" actually means**

You don't need new information for this. You need the pieces already covered, put in one place:

The panel from Chapters 5 and 6: fasting glucose, A1C, fasting insulin, and a calculated HOMA-IR, so you're tracking more than the two numbers your last physical happened to order. Food order from Chapter 9, the single best-evidenced habit in this book for someone in your exact population. The muscle connection from Chapter 8: structured exercise isn't optional here, it works through a mechanism that doesn't wait on insulin at all. Sleep from Chapter 10, which moves insulin sensitivity by a measurable amount within days, not months. And a retest on a schedule you actually know, rather than a vague someday: ask your prescriber directly what interval they recommend for you and why, the same way Chapter 6 suggested asking for the panel itself.

**What "watching it" is worth, done right**

"Watch it" done well is not passive. It's the same protocol, in miniature, that produced a 58% reduction in a landmark trial, applied by you, starting now, for as long as you keep applying it. That's a genuinely different thing to hear than "your doctor didn't think this was worth treating."

The next chapter is for a different situation entirely: what changes for your blood sugar if you're already taking a GLP-1 medication.

> **Short Version**
>
> The medication is already doing much of what the earlier chapters describe, by lowering glucose directly. What it doesn't do is protect muscle: real-world and randomized data both show lean mass falling alongside fat, within five weeks in one crossover trial. *Your Body's Own GLP-1* covers the drug decision itself: what these medications do, what they cost, whether to start, escalate, stop, or resume. That question belongs there, and this chapter will not repeat it. What belongs here is narrower: what happens to your blood sugar specifically, while you're taking one, and what happens to it if you stop.

**The drug is already doing most of what this book is about**

GLP-1 medications were developed as diabetes drugs before they became known for weight loss, and lowering blood glucose is a core part of how they work. If you're taking one, much of what earlier chapters describe, the compensation your pancreas was quietly running, the gap between a normal fasting glucose and real insulin resistance, is being actively addressed by the medication itself, not left to a food-order habit or a walk after dinner to fix alone.

**What the drug doesn't cover: muscle**

A real-world study of 94 adults, 86% of them women, averaging 46 years old, tracked body composition over three months of treatment with a compounded semaglutide and vitamin B12 injection at a commercial wellness clinic. Meaningful fat loss came with it, and lean muscle mass rose slightly as a proportion of total body weight, but the absolute number told a different story: participants lost close to a kilogram of skeletal muscle mass along with the fat. **[associated only]** A real-world retrospective cohort at a single wellness studio, not a randomized trial, and the compounded formulation is not identical to the FDA-approved semaglutide brands, though the muscle-loss finding matches what's seen in placebo-controlled trials of the approved drug elsewhere in this chapter.

A more tightly controlled study makes the same point over a much shorter window. Twenty adults with obesity took liraglutide or placebo for 35 days each, in a randomized crossover design. Absolute lean mass in the trunk and limbs measurably decreased on the drug, even though the fat-free mass *percentage* held steady, because total weight was dropping too. **[shown in people]** A short randomized crossover trial; the muscle loss appeared within five weeks, not years.

That's exactly the ground Chapter 8 already covered: muscle is a primary site of glucose disposal, one your body relies on independent of insulin. Losing it while a medication is doing the glucose-lowering work for you doesn't show up as a problem now. It becomes one later, if the medication stops. Protecting that muscle while you're on the drug isn't a side project. It's protection for the version of your blood sugar that has to stand on its own again someday. That is a full book's own work, and *The Muscle You Keep* is where it's done properly; this chapter isn't the place to attempt it in miniature.

**What happens if you stop**

This is the part with the least discussion attached to it, and it matters directly for blood sugar. A follow-up of the STEP 1 trial tracked 327 participants for a year after they stopped taking semaglutide, following 68 weeks of treatment that had produced substantial weight loss. By one year off the drug, participants had regained roughly two-thirds of the weight they'd lost, and the cardiometabolic improvements from the treatment period, glucose among them, reverted back toward where they'd started. **[shown in people]** A randomized trial extension, and one of the more directly relevant findings in this entire book for anyone weighing how long to stay on a GLP-1 medication.

None of that is an argument for or against staying on the medication; that decision sits with you and your prescriber, exactly where *Your Body's Own GLP-1* leaves it. What it does mean is that whatever blood-sugar gains the drug is producing right now aren't banked permanently the moment you stop. The habits from the rest of this book, food order, muscle, sleep, are what carry the glucose picture forward on their own if the medication isn't there to carry it for you.

**What to do with this**

Ask your prescriber how the drug itself is affecting your glucose numbers specifically, since that's information this book can't hand you directly. Read *The Muscle You Keep* for the actual protein and training plan if losing lean mass while on the medication concerns you. And treat the rest of this book as insurance for the day the prescription changes, not as advice about whether it should.

### Chapter 14: If You're Perimenopausal and Everything Feels Like Guessing

> **Short Version**
>
> Estrogen through perimenopause doesn't decline in a straight line. It swings, sometimes above your own earlier baseline before dropping again, and insulin sensitivity is reasoned to move with it, though no study has directly tracked the two together in the same women over time. A fasting glucose or A1C drawn during one stretch can read differently from the same test a few months later, with nothing else in your life changed. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR move more slowly than fasting glucose, which may make them steadier reference points through the transition.

Perimenopause resists a clean before-and-after story. Cycles shift, then don't, then do again. A symptom shows up for two months and vanishes. A lab drawn on one visit reads differently than the same test six months later, and neither reading is wrong; the biology underneath is genuinely in motion. If you've felt like you can't get a stable read on your own body lately, that instinct is accurate, not a failure of attention.

**Why the ground is actually moving**

Chapter 3 covered the mechanism: estrogen has a real, traceable role in keeping insulin working efficiently, and that role weakens as estrogen production becomes erratic through the transition, not simply low. Erratic is the operative word. Estrogen during perimenopause doesn't decline in a smooth line; it swings, sometimes higher than a woman's own baseline before dropping again, on a timeline that varies from cycle to cycle. A body's insulin sensitivity, tied to that hormone, moves along with it. A fasting glucose or A1C drawn during a higher-estrogen stretch can look different from one drawn a few months later during a lower stretch, in the same woman, without anything else in her life changing. **[not studied]** The estrogen fluctuation pattern itself is well documented, and Chapter 3 already covered the mechanism connecting it to insulin sensitivity. But no study has directly tracked glucose against an individual woman's own hormonal swings over time. This claim is reasoning stitched together from that other evidence, not a finding drawn from a study of its own.

**What to actually do with a moving target**

Don't anchor to one lab draw. A single fasting glucose or A1C, taken during perimenopause, is a snapshot of a body in transition, not a verdict. If a number surprises you, high or unexpectedly normal, a repeat draw a few months later tells you more than either single number alone.

Use the full panel from Chapters 5 and 6, not just the two numbers a standard visit happens to order. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR move more slowly and more informatively than fasting glucose alone, which makes them a steadier reference point while everything else is in flux.

Track your own symptom pattern alongside the labs, not instead of them. Chapter 10 already covered how disrupted sleep in this transition, driven partly by hormonal effects on the airway itself and not only by hot flashes, can independently worsen insulin sensitivity on its own timeline. A stretch of bad sleep and a stretch of higher glucose showing up together isn't a coincidence to dismiss; it's two pieces of the same mechanism.

And bring a clinician who takes the transition itself seriously, not one who treats every question as premature until a final menstrual period is twelve months behind you. The biology described in Chapter 3 is active years before that marker arrives, and your blood sugar doesn't wait for an official finish line to start responding to it.

**The instability itself is the finding**

The instability itself is the finding, not a gap in your self-tracking. Expect variation, plan for repeat testing rather than a single number, and lean on the fuller panel and the symptom pattern together, since either one alone is easier to misread than the two combined.

## Appendix E: Glossary

**A1C (HbA1c).** A blood test measuring the percentage of hemoglobin with glucose stuck to it, giving a rolling average of blood sugar over roughly three months. See Chapter 5.

**AMPK.** An enzyme inside muscle cells that senses energy use and, among other jobs, helps pull glucose into the cell during exercise, independent of insulin. See Chapter 8.

**Beta cell.** The cell type in the pancreas that produces and releases insulin.

**BMI (body mass index).** A ratio of weight to height, used in most screening guidelines as a rough proxy for body composition. It does not distinguish muscle from fat.

**Confidence interval (CI).** A range that a study's true result most likely falls within. A narrower range means a more precise estimate; a range that crosses zero (or 1, for a ratio) usually means the finding wasn't statistically significant.

**Continuous glucose monitor (CGM).** A small sensor, usually worn on the arm, that measures glucose in the fluid under the skin every few minutes. See Chapter 7.

**Cross-over trial.** A study design where the same participants go through more than one condition (for example, both a treatment and a placebo), each acting as their own comparison. Generally stronger than comparing two separate groups of people.

**Fasting insulin.** A blood test measuring insulin at a single fasting moment, revealing how hard the pancreas is working to hold glucose steady. See Chapter 5.

**GLP-1.** A hormone made naturally by the gut that affects appetite, insulin release, and blood sugar; also the class of medications (semaglutide, tirzepatide, and others) that mimic or extend its effect. See Chapter 13 and *Your Body's Own GLP-1*.

**GLUT4.** The transporter protein that physically carries glucose across a cell's membrane and into the cell, in both muscle and fat tissue. See Chapter 8.

**GMI (Glucose Management Indicator).** A consumer CGM app's estimate of your A1C, calculated from sensor readings rather than a blood draw. See Chapter 7 for how well this estimate holds up.

**HOMA-IR.** A calculated score (fasting glucose multiplied by fasting insulin, divided by a constant) estimating insulin resistance. See Chapter 5.

**Insulin resistance.** A state in which cells respond less readily to insulin's signal, requiring the pancreas to produce more of it to achieve the same effect.

**Meta-analysis.** A study that statistically pools the results of multiple separate trials on the same question, to produce one combined estimate.

**Meta-regression.** A meta-analysis that also tests whether specific factors (age, a program component, a dose) explain differences in outcome across the pooled trials.

**Network meta-analysis.** A meta-analysis that compares several different treatments at once, even when not every treatment was tested head-to-head in the same trial, by combining direct and indirect evidence.

**Odds ratio (OR).** A measure of how much more (or less) likely an outcome is in one group versus another. An OR of 1.40 means the outcome was 40% more likely in the group being described.

**OGTT (oral glucose tolerance test).** A test that measures blood glucose before and after drinking a standardized glucose solution, showing how the body handles a real glucose load over one to two hours. See Chapter 5.

**Person-years.** A way of combining the number of people studied with how long they were followed. A rate of "35 per 1,000 person-years" describes how often an event happened across that combined total of observation time.

**Prediabetes.** A lab range between normal and diabetic glucose control, defined differently by different medical bodies. See Chapter 2.

**Randomized controlled trial (RCT).** A study that randomly assigns participants to a treatment or a comparison group, the strongest common design for testing whether something actually causes an effect.

**Relative risk (RR).** A ratio comparing the probability of an outcome in one group to another. An RR of 0.40 means the outcome was 60% less likely in the group being described.

**Systematic review.** A study that gathers and evaluates all the available research on a specific question, using a defined, reproducible search method.

## Appendix G: Glucose Unit Conversion

Most U.S. labs report glucose in mg/dL; most research outside the U.S., and some international studies cited in this book, report it in mmol/L. To convert mg/dL to mmol/L, divide by 18. To convert mmol/L to mg/dL, multiply by 18.

| mg/dL (U.S. standard) | mmol/L |
|---|---|
| 70 | 3.9 |
| 100 | 5.6 |
| 110 | 6.1 |
| 125 | 6.9 |
| 140 | 7.8 |
| 180 | 10.0 |
| 200 | 11.1 |

A1C is reported as a percentage in both systems and needs no conversion.
