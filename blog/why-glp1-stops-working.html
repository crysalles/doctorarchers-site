Why Your Own GLP-1 Seems to Stop Working

August 1, 2026

Written and reviewed by Rev. Dr. LaVeena B. Archers, PhD
Doctor of Natural Medicine · Board-certified in Holistic Functional Medicine and Functional Nutrition by a private credentialing board
Educational content only. Not medical advice, and never a substitute for your own care. Scope of practice.

The women who write to me often describe the same change. They did everything right for years, and somewhere in their forties the fullness that used to arrive after a meal stopped arriving. Hunger came back sooner. The old habits stopped holding their weight. Many of them ask the same question: has my own GLP-1 stopped working? It is a fair question, and the honest answer surprises people. For most women, GLP-1 did not break. It got blunted.

What "blunted" means

Your body still makes GLP-1 after meals. The gut cells that release it are still there, still doing their job. What changes is how strong the signal is, and how well your brain hears it. A blunted signal is a quieter one. It still fires, it just does not carry the same weight it once did, so the message to stop eating arrives late, or softly, or not at all. That is a very different problem from a broken hormone, and the difference matters, because a quieter signal can usually be turned back up.

Why the signal gets quieter

There are five common reasons, and most women are living with more than one at a time.

Modern food is absorbed too high, and too fast. Much of your GLP-1 comes from cells low in the small intestine and the colon. They release the most when food, especially fiber and protein, actually reaches them. Highly processed food is broken down and absorbed near the top of the gut, quickly, so it never travels far enough to wake those lower cells. You eat, but the part of the gut that makes the strongest fullness signal barely hears about it.

A starved gut makes a weaker signal. The bacteria in your lower gut ferment fiber into short-chain fatty acids, and those are one of the things that prompt your cells to release GLP-1 and its partner hormone PYY. A diet low in fiber starves those bacteria, and a quieter gut community means a quieter hormone. This is one of the most fixable pieces of the whole picture.

Blood sugar on a roller coaster wears the system down. When meals are built around fast carbohydrates, blood sugar spikes and drops, insulin stays high, and over time the body responds less well to its own signals, appetite hormones included. Steadier blood sugar is part of what lets GLP-1 be heard again.

Short sleep and a nervous system stuck on. Poor sleep and constant stress push hunger and fullness hormones in the wrong direction, raising the drive to eat and dulling the signal that says enough. This is not about willpower. A tired, stressed body is working against its own appetite brakes.

Falling estrogen, in midlife. Here I want to be careful about what is actually known. In female animals, estrogen appears to sharpen the brain's response to GLP-1, so the same amount of the hormone curbs appetite more when estrogen is present and less when it runs low. Whether that carries over into women has not been shown. If it does, the drop in estrogen through perimenopause could leave your own GLP-1 a little less able to quiet appetite, in the very season when appetite already feels harder to read. I offer that as a plausible piece of the puzzle, not a settled fact.

What the drugs actually do

This is where the medicines fit. Semaglutide and tirzepatide do not repair any of the conditions above. What they do is flood the body with a long-lasting version of the GLP-1 signal and hold it on, at a strength your own gut cannot reach, regardless of what your food, your gut, your sleep, or your hormones are doing. That is why they work even when the natural signal has gone quiet. It is also why the effect fades when people stop, because the drug was holding the signal up while the underlying conditions stayed the same. For some women, especially those carrying real health risk, that steady signal is a legitimate and useful tool, and any decision about it belongs with your own prescriber.

Turning your own signal back up

If the signal is blunted rather than broken, the work is to raise it, and the levers are ordinary ones. None of this matches a pharmaceutical dose, and all of it is real, and it supports your health far beyond this one hormone:

  • Protein and fiber at every meal, the two things that prompt the strongest GLP-1 response
  • Whole food your gut recognizes, so it reaches the lower gut that makes the signal
  • A well-fed gut community, tended with fiber and fermented foods
  • Steadier blood sugar, by building meals around protein, fiber, and fat rather than fast carbohydrates
  • Movement most days, and strength work that protects the muscle your appetite hormones respond to
  • Sleep and a settled nervous system, treated as real work rather than a luxury

Given time, these do the thing a drug cannot. They raise your own signal, at the source.

Start from the more likely truth

So the next time you wonder whether your own GLP-1 has quit, start from what is most likely. It is probably still there, just quieter than it was, for reasons that make sense once you can see them.
