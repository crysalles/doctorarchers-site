Homeopathy's Place in Holistic Health, Honestly

August 7, 2026

Written and reviewed by Rev. Dr. LaVeena B. Archers, PhD
Doctor of Natural Medicine · Board-certified in Holistic Functional Medicine and Functional Nutrition
Educational content only. Not medical advice, and never a substitute for your own care. Scope of practice.

Quick answers

Does the largest independent evidence support homeopathy?
No. Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council reviewed 57 systematic reviews covering 176 trials in 2015 and found no reliable evidence homeopathy is effective for any health condition [3]. A fresh, independent evaluation in March 2025 examined dozens of studies across 25 priority conditions and reached the same conclusion [4].
Is homeopathy dangerous?
Properly diluted homeopathic remedies carry very little risk, but "homeopathic" on a label is not automatically a guarantee of proper dilution. In 2016 and 2017 the FDA investigated homeopathic teething products found to contain inconsistent, sometimes measurable amounts of a toxic plant extract that was supposed to be diluted to nothing [6].
When should you see a doctor instead of trying a remedy?
Any high fever, difficulty breathing, chest pain, signs of a serious infection, a wound that needs stitches, symptoms of a stroke or heart attack, a mental health emergency, or any condition your doctor is actively treating and monitoring call for real medical care, not a remedy from a health food store.

The largest and most independent evaluations of homeopathy have not found reliable evidence that it works. Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council reviewed 57 systematic reviews covering 176 individual trials in 2015 and concluded there was no reliable evidence that homeopathy is effective for treating any health condition [3]; a fresh, independent evaluation published in March 2025 across 25 priority conditions again found low to very low certainty evidence and little to no benefit over placebo [4]. Properly manufactured remedies also carry very little risk. That combination, a real absence of proof paired with a real absence of danger, is homeopathy's honest place in holistic health: a low-cost option some people find comforting for minor complaints, and not a plan for anything serious.

I met homeopathy about thirty years ago, through my massage therapy teacher. She handed me a box of Oscillococcinum, a widely sold flu remedy, during a bad bout of flu-like symptoms. It was cheap, it required no appointment, and it gave me something to do while I waited to feel better. That box is the reason I have spent three decades curious about this field.

It is also, as it turns out, a good place to start an honest look at the evidence. Oscillococcinum has been tested more than most homeopathic products, in six randomized, placebo-controlled trials. A 2015 Cochrane review of those trials found no significant difference between Oscillococcinum and placebo for preventing flu-like illness, and only a modest, short-lived effect on symptom relief in the two treatment trials good enough to analyze, an effect that had faded by day four [5]. The Cochrane reviewers were careful to say the evidence was too thin to draw a firm conclusion either way, not that the product had been proven useless. They also found no evidence of meaningful harm. That mix, a real absence of proof paired with a real absence of danger, describes most of what follows here.

What does the research on homeopathy actually show?

The honest picture is a genuine split between a small number of statistically positive meta-analyses and a much larger, more rigorous body of review finding no reliable effect at all.

On the positive side: a 1997 meta-analysis in The Lancet pooled 89 placebo-controlled trials and found an overall effect favoring homeopathy, an effect that shrank when the analysis was limited to the 26 best-quality trials, and that the authors themselves said was not strong enough to call homeopathy clearly effective for any single condition [1]. Two years later, a follow-up analysis by much of the same research team looked specifically at what happened to that effect as trial quality improved, and found that better-designed studies consistently produced smaller, less positive results [2]. In other words, the more carefully a trial was run, the less homeopathy outperformed placebo.

On the other side of the ledger sit the largest, most independent evaluations. Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council reviewed 57 systematic reviews covering 176 individual trials in 2015 and concluded there was no reliable evidence that homeopathy is effective for treating any health condition [3]. Homeopathy advocates argued the review's inclusion criteria were unusually strict and excluded much of the published literature. That criticism is real, and stating it rather than burying it is the standard this site runs on. It did not change the outcome: when the NHMRC returned to the question in a fresh, independent evaluation published in March 2025, examining dozens of studies across 25 priority conditions, it again found evidence of low to very low certainty and little to no benefit over placebo for most outcomes assessed [4]. Two independent reviews, a decade apart, reached the same conclusion.

Is homeopathy ever dangerous?

Homeopathy's safety record has one real exception worth knowing. In 2016 and 2017, the FDA warned that some homeopathic teething tablets and gels contained inconsistent, sometimes measurable amounts of belladonna, a toxic plant extract that is supposed to be diluted to nothing. The agency received reports of seizures in infants and was investigating ten infant deaths in children who had used these products, though it said it could not confirm the products caused the deaths, and the manufacturer recalled them nationwide [6]. The lesson is not that homeopathy is secretly dangerous. It is that "homeopathic" on a label is not a guarantee of proper dilution, and any product intended for an infant deserves the same scrutiny you would give any other medicine.

Where does homeopathy fit, and where does it not?

None of the evidence above means homeopathy is fraud, and none of it means it is medicine in the sense that word usually carries. Given how little most remedies cost and how little risk they carry when properly manufactured, using one for a head cold, a minor bruise, or a rough night's sleep is a reasonable personal choice. What the evidence does not support is using homeopathy as a plan for anything serious: a chronic autoimmune condition, an infection that needs antibiotics, a mental health crisis, or any symptom that is getting worse rather than better.

When to see a clinician instead. Any high fever, difficulty breathing, chest pain, signs of a serious infection, a wound that needs stitches, symptoms of a stroke or heart attack, a mental health emergency, or any condition your doctor is actively treating and monitoring: these call for real medical care, not a remedy from a health food store. If you use a homeopathic product for a minor complaint and it has not helped within a day or two, or if things are getting worse, that is your sign to call a doctor rather than try a different remedy.

Homeopathy's place in holistic health is a modest one: a low-cost, low-risk option some people find comforting for the kind of minor complaints that were often going to resolve on their own. I would rather name it clearly than dress it up as more than the evidence supports.
