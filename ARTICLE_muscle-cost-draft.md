# The Muscle Cost of the Weight-Loss Shots: What the Warnings Get Right, and Where They Go Too Far

A video is making the rounds. A doctor, alarmed, warning that Ozempic and the drugs like it are creating "skinny-fat" people, eating away their muscle, and setting older adults up for a metabolic disaster. The language is dire. Class-action lawsuits. An epidemic. A death sentence for the elderly.

I want to do something this topic rarely gets: sort the part that is genuinely true and important from the part that is fear turned up for effect. Because underneath the alarm sits a real concern, and it is the same one I wrote an entire book about.

## What the warning gets right

The muscle point is true, and it is the cost of these medicines that gets the least attention.

When you lose weight quickly, by any method, you never lose only fat. You lose some muscle and some bone along with it. On these medicines, studies show that a meaningful share of the weight a person loses, often around a quarter and sometimes more, is lean mass. For a young person with muscle to spare, that matters less. For a woman in or past midlife, it is a different story. Muscle is the tissue that keeps you strong, keeps your metabolism steady, and keeps you on your feet as the years go on. Losing it carelessly, in exchange for a smaller number on the scale, is a trade very few women would make if someone laid it out for them at the start.

So the "Ozempic face" people talk about, and the softer, weaker body some end up with, are real. They are a sign of muscle and bone leaving. For older adults the loss matters even more, because the slow loss of muscle with age, called sarcopenia, is one of the real drivers of frailty later in life.

The warning gets the deeper point right too. A drug that switches off your appetite does not fix why your appetite stopped working in the first place. Skip the root causes, the food, the insulin, the sleep, the movement, the muscle, and you have covered a symptom, not solved a problem.

## Where it goes too far

Three places.

**"If you are overweight, you are insulin resistant."** Most people carrying extra weight do have some degree of insulin resistance. Not all of them do, and stating it as a certainty for everyone is not accurate.

**"You must be in ketosis before you touch these drugs."** That is one doctor's protocol, not settled medicine. A ketogenic diet helps some people a great deal, and it is not the only way. You can protect your muscle with enough protein and regular resistance training, steadier blood sugar, and better sleep, whether or not you are formally in ketosis. There is no single method you have to follow.

**The tone.** Death sentence. Epidemic. Malpractice. Fear gets clicks, and it also clouds judgment. These medicines carry real costs and real risks, and for the right person, when health is genuinely at stake, they can also be a real help. Both of those are true at the same time. A woman deciding about her own body deserves the full picture in proportion, not whoever is most alarmed.

## What to actually do, on the drug or off it

The most important cost of these medicines is largely protectable. And the same habits protect you whether you ever take one or not.

**Protein, on purpose.** Eat real protein at every meal, even when your appetite has fallen so low that eating feels like a chore. On these medicines it takes real intention, because the appetite that once made you overeat is now working against your muscle.

**Resistance training, without fail.** Ask your muscles to work against real load a couple of times a week. Muscle that is used is muscle the body is far more reluctant to give up.

**Work the root, not just the symptom.** Steady your blood sugar, tend your sleep and your stress, and feed the gut that makes your own appetite hormone. Your body already makes GLP-1. Supporting your own is the foundation, drug or no drug.

**Measure more than the scale.** Watch your strength, your energy, how your clothes fit, and, with your own prescriber, the markers under the surface. The scale can fall while muscle leaves. Treat it as one of several measures, never as the judge of your whole health.

## Where this leaves you

The video is right that too many people are being handed these drugs with no plan to protect their muscle, and that this is a real, under-discussed harm, sharpest for women in midlife. It is wrong to turn that into a verdict that the drugs are poison and that one strict diet is the only safe way through.

Your body already makes the hormone these drugs imitate. Medicine or not, the work that protects you is the same: protect your muscle, support your own GLP-1, and keep the scale in its place among the measures that matter more.
